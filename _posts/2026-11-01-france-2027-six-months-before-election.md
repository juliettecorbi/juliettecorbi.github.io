In six months, France will elect its new president. The election is seen as crucial: Marine Le Pen, candidate of the far-right Rassemblement National, currently leads in voting intentions. Yet there are still six months to go, and a lot can change.

Over the coming months, I will analyse the situation in France here and on LinkedIn, combining a political science perspective with my knowledge and experience as a French person. I originally planned to focus only on the presidential campaign, but I may also address democratic backsliding, which we are currently observing in France -- not only from far-right forces, but also from our government.

But first, some key facts to understand how France works, as both its presidential election and its executive--legislative system are quite specific.

The presidential election determines who holds executive power, and it takes place in two rounds. The first round determines which two candidates receive the most votes and advance to the second. In the second round, voters choose again between these two candidates: the one they prefer, or, as has been the case lately, the one they fear the least. This is how Emmanuel Macron won a majority of the votes cast in the last elections: not necessarily because most voters supported his political views, but because they chose the lesser evil (that said, Macron did benefit, especially in 2017, from a solid base of supporters). It also means that leading the first round is no guarantee of winning the second.

Currently, the main candidates are Marine Le Pen, Jean-Luc Mélenchon (candidate of the left-wing party LFI) and Édouard Philippe (candidate of Horizon, a party close to Emmanuel Macron's). According to the latest Ipsos poll, Le Pen is at around 33% of first-round voting intentions, Mélenchon at 17% and Philippe at around 15% when he is the only candidate of the central bloc, with Raphaël Glucksmann not far behind (13--15.5%, depending on the scenario).

Yet we are still six months away from the first round, and things are moving fast, as current events in France show: social protests, violence, the authoritarian turn of the state, a racist terrorist attack. These events strongly shape French people's vote in 2027 and may already be having an effect. The Ipsos poll was conducted on 21--22 October, after the protests of 17 October: Le Pen remains at her September level (slightly lower in some scenarios), while Mélenchon gains 2 points since September, continuing the upward trend of recent months, and Philippe loses up to 4 points.

A lot can still change before the vote, and later events may not yet be reflected in the polls. For example, the racist terrorist attack that resulted in the death of one person, Pengo Bossio Wangi, and four severely injured people, among whom a 12-year-old girl, will have an effect on voting intentions, likely decreasing support for Rassemblement National, which has not been shown by the polls yet.

As mentioned, I will analyse these dynamics in the weeks and months to come, so stay tuned.

*Source: Ipsos bva--CESI École d'ingénieurs for Le Parisien, fieldwork 21--22 October 2026, published 24 October 2026 ([ipsos.com](https://www.ipsos.com/fr-fr/presidentielle-2027-melenchon-progresse-le-pen-reste-en-tete)).*
