In six months, France will elect its new president. The election is seen as crucial: Marine Le Pen, candidate of the far-right Rassemblement National (RN), currently leads the voting intentions. Yet there are still six months to go, and a lot can change.

Over the coming months, I will analyse the situation in France here, combining a political science perspective with my knowledge as a French person. I originally planned to focus on the presidential campaign, but I may also address other topics such as democratic backsliding, which we are currently observing in France from our government, as well as questions on social protest.

But first, some key facts to understand how France works, as both its presidential election and its executive--legislative system are quite specific.

The presidential election determines who holds executive power, and it takes place in two rounds. The first round determines which two candidates received the most votes and advance to the second. In the second round, voters choose again between these two candidates -- often the one they fear least. This is how Emmanuel Macron won a majority of the votes cast in the last elections: not necessarily because most voters supported his political views, but because they chose the lesser evil (that said, Macron did benefit, especially in 2017, from a solid base of supporters). It also means that leading the first round is no guarantee of winning the second.

Currently, the main candidates are Marine Le Pen, Jean-Luc Mélenchon (candidate of the left-wing party LFI) and Édouard Philippe (candidate of Horizon, a party close to Emmanuel Macron's). According to the latest Ipsos poll, Le Pen is at around 33% of first-round voting intentions, Mélenchon at 17% and Philippe at around 15% when he is the only candidate of the central bloc, with Raphaël Glucksmann not far behind (13--15.5%, depending on the scenario).

Yet we are still six months away from the first round, and things are moving fast, as current events in France show: social protests, violence, the authoritarian turn of the state, a racist terrorist attack. These events strongly shape French people's vote in 2027 and may already be having an effect. The Ipsos poll was conducted on 21--22 October, after the violence of 17 October: Le Pen remains at her September level (slightly lower in some scenarios), while Mélenchon gains 2 points since September, continuing the upward trend of recent months, and Philippe loses up to 4 points.

A lot can still change before the vote, and later events may not yet be reflected in the polls. Moreover, polls do not always precisely reflect voting intentions or accurately capture the political situation. As mentioned, I will analyse these dynamics in the weeks and months to come, so stay tuned!
